\begin{frame}{Why look beyond the syllabus?}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item This course teaches you \textbf{tools}: asymptotic analysis, greedy algorithms,
    divide and conquer, dynamic programming, network flow, NP-completeness.
    \item These tools are not museum pieces. Researchers are actively using them
    \textbf{right now} to settle questions that were open for 40--60 years.
    \item Today's supplement has two parts:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item A tour of the \textbf{complexity zoo} -- far more classes than just P and

      worked example, that broke a decades-old speed barrier using exactly the kind

  \end{itemize}
  \begin{alertblock}{The message}
    ``Textbook algorithm'' does not mean ``final answer.'' Dijkstra's algorithm is 70 years
    old and still generating Symposium-on-Foundations-of-Computer-Science best-paper awards.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\section{The Complexity Zoo}
% =================================================================

\begin{frame}{What is a complexity class?}
  \begin{block}{Definition (informal)}
    A \textbf{complexity class} is a set of computational problems that can be solved
    using a certain \emph{amount} of a certain \emph{resource} -- as a function of input
    size $n$.
  \end{block}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The resource is not always \textbf{time}. It can be \textbf{space} (memory),
    \textbf{randomness}, or even the number of \textbf{quantifier alternations} in a
    logical formula -- each gives a genuinely different classification of problems.
    \item P and NP (Week 12 preview) are the two most famous classes, but they are just
    two rooms in a much bigger zoo. Today we visit six more rooms: \textbf{L, NL, coNP,
    PSPACE, BPP/RP/ZPP, and \#P}.
    \item Why bother? If a problem is provably outside P, you stop hunting for a fast
    exact algorithm and pivot -- to approximation (Week 14), randomization (Week 15), or
    exponential-but-practical search.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{P and EXP: the two time-based anchors}
  \begin{block}{P (Polynomial time)}
    Decision problems solvable in $O(n^k)$ time, for some constant $k$.
  \end{block}
  \begin{block}{EXP (Exponential time)}
    Decision problems solvable in $O(2^{n^k})$ time, for some constant $k$.
  \end{block}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    $n$ & $n^2$ (P-ish) & $n^5$ (P-ish) & $2^n$ (EXP-ish) & $n!$ (worse than EXP) \\
    \midrule
    $10$ & $100$ & $10^5$ & $1{,}024$ & $3.6\times 10^6$ \\
    $20$ & $400$ & $3.2\times10^6$ & $\approx 10^6$ & $2.4\times10^{18}$ \\
    $50$ & $2{,}500$ & $3.1\times10^8$ & $\approx 10^{15}$ & (astronomical) \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Shortest paths, sorting, matching, network flow -- almost everything in
    Weeks 3--11 lives in P. A classical diagonalization argument proves
    $\mathbf{P \ne EXP}$: \emph{something} in this zoo is provably hard, even though we
    can't always pin down \emph{which familiar problem} it is.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Space matters too: L, NL, and PSPACE}
  \begin{block}{L and NL (Logarithmic space)}
    \textbf{L}: decision problems solvable using only $O(\log n)$ bits of \emph{working
    memory} (the input itself is read-only and doesn't count). \textbf{NL}: the same,
    but the algorithm may guess (nondeterminism).
  \end{block}
  \begin{block}{PSPACE (Polynomial space)}
    Decision problems solvable using $O(n^k)$ bits of working memory -- with
    \textbf{no limit on time} at all.
  \end{block}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $O(\log n)$ memory is enough to store a handful of vertex labels or counters,
    but not an entire path or the whole input -- a much tighter budget than
    ``polynomial time.''
    \item Known: $\mathbf{L \subseteq NL \subseteq P \subseteq PSPACE \subseteq EXP}$.
    Whether $L = NL$ is open -- a space-world cousin of $P$ vs.\ $NP$.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked example: \textsc{Path} is NL-complete}
  \begin{columns}
  \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[
      node distance=1.3cm and 1.6cm,
      vtx/.style={circle, draw, thick, minimum size=7mm},
      >=Stealth]
    \node[vtx] (s) {$s$};
    \node[vtx, right=of s] (a) {$a$};
    \node[vtx, below=of s] (c) {$c$};
    \node[vtx, right=of a] (t) {$t$};
    \node[vtx, below=of c] (d) {$d$};
    \draw[->, thick] (s) -- (a);
    \draw[->, thick] (a) -- (t);
    \draw[->, thick] (s) -- (c);
    \draw[->, thick] (c) -- (d);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}
  \end{column}
  \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
  \textbf{Question:} is there a directed path from $s$ to $t$?
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Path $s \to a \to t$ exists: \textbf{yes}.
    \item A logspace algorithm never stores the whole path -- only the
    \textbf{current vertex} and a \textbf{step counter} ($O(\log n)$ bits each).
    \item \emph{Nondeterministically} guess the next vertex at each step; accept if $t$
    is reached within $n$ steps.
  \end{itemize}
  \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{exampleblock}{A genuine surprise}
    Immerman and Szelepcs\'enyi (1987--88) proved $\mathbf{NL = coNL}$: nondeterministic
    logspace is closed under complement -- \emph{despite} nondeterminism seeming
    ``one-directional.'' It took 20 years after NL was defined to discover this.
  \end{exampleblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{NP: efficiently \emph{verifiable} problems}
  \begin{block}{NP (Nondeterministic Polynomial time)}
    Decision problems where, \emph{if the answer is ``yes,''} there is a certificate
    that can be \textbf{checked} in polynomial time.
  \end{block}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Every problem in P is also in NP: if you can \emph{solve} it fast, you can
    certainly \emph{verify} a solution fast. So $\mathbf{P \subseteq NP}$.
    \item The single biggest open question in computer science: \textbf{is $P = NP$?}
    Can everything easy to \emph{check} also be made easy to \emph{solve}?
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked example: verifying a Subset-Sum certificate}
  \textbf{Instance:} $S = \{3, 34, 4, 12, 5, 2\}$, target $T = 9$. \textbf{Question:}
  does some subset of $S$ sum to exactly $T$?
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Solving} this from scratch means searching (in the worst known case)
    over all $2^{|S|}$ subsets -- exponential.
    \item But someone can hand you a \textbf{certificate}: the subset $\{4, 5\}$.
  \end{itemize}
  \[
    4 + 5 = 9 = T. \quad \checkmark
  \]
  \begin{exampleblock}{The point of NP}
    Checking the certificate took one addition and one comparison: $O(|S|)$ time,
    regardless of how hard it was to \emph{find} $\{4,5\}$ in the first place. That gap
    between ``hard to find'' and ``easy to check'' is exactly what NP captures.
  \end{exampleblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{NP-complete and NP-hard}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item A problem is \textbf{NP-hard} if every problem in NP reduces to it in
    polynomial time -- ``at least as hard as anything in NP.''
    \item A problem is \textbf{NP-complete} if it is NP-hard \emph{and} itself in NP --
    the ``hardest problems inside NP.''
    \item Cook and Levin (1971) showed \textsc{SAT} (Boolean satisfiability) is
    NP-complete. Thousands of problems are now known to be NP-complete, including
    \textbf{Subset Sum} itself, the \textbf{Traveling Salesman Problem} (decision
    version), \textsc{3-Coloring}, and \textsc{Vertex Cover} -- formalized in Week 12.
  \end{itemize}
  \begin{exampleblock}{Why this matters in practice}
    Reduce a known NP-complete problem to \emph{your} problem, and you have strong
    evidence it has \textbf{no} polynomial-time exact algorithm (unless $P=NP$) -- your
    cue to switch strategies.
  \end{exampleblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{coNP: the mirror image of NP}
  \begin{block}{coNP}
    The class of problems whose \textbf{``no''} answers have polynomial-time-checkable
    certificates. Formally: $L \in coNP \iff \overline{L} \in NP$.
  \end{block}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Example: \textsc{Tautology} -- ``is this Boolean formula true under
    \emph{every} assignment?'' A ``no'' certificate is a single falsifying assignment,
    e.g.\ for $\varphi = (x \lor y) \land (\lnot x \lor \lnot y)$, the assignment
    $x{=}y{=}\text{true}$ falsifies it -- checkable instantly.
    \item But a ``yes'' certificate would seem to require checking \emph{all}
    $2^n$ assignments -- no shortcut is known.
    \item Believed (not proven) that $\mathbf{NP \ne coNP}$: SAT's ``no'' instances
    (unsatisfiability) are not believed to have short certificates either.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The big picture (time and space classes)}
  \begin{center}

  \begin{itemize}
    \item Known for sure: $\mathbf{L \subseteq NL \subseteq P \subseteq NP \subseteq
    PSPACE \subseteq EXP}$. Not shown to scale: \textbf{coNP} (NP's mirror, conjectured
    $\ne$ NP), \textbf{BPP/RP/ZPP} (randomized, next), \textbf{\#P} (counting, later).
    \item \textbf{Not} known: whether any inclusion above is \emph{strict}, except
    $P \ne EXP$.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked example: quantifiers and PSPACE}
  \textsc{TQBF} (True Quantified Boolean Formula) is the canonical
  \textbf{PSPACE-complete} problem: decide if a fully-quantified Boolean formula is
  true.
  \[
    \varphi \;=\; \exists x\, \forall y \; (x \lor y)
  \]
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Try $x = \text{true}$: then $\forall y\,(\text{true} \lor y)$ is true for
    \emph{every} $y$ (true, since $\text{true} \lor \text{anything} = \text{true}$).
    \item So $x = \text{true}$ witnesses the outer $\exists$: $\varphi$ is
    \textbf{true}. $\checkmark$
  \end{itemize}
  \begin{exampleblock}{Why this is PSPACE, not just NP}
    Each quantifier can be evaluated by \emph{reusing} the same working memory for the
    next one (recursion on formula structure), so only $O(n)$ space is needed --
    but potentially exponentially many assignments get explored over time.
    \textbf{Savitch's theorem} (1970) shows nondeterministic and deterministic
    polynomial-\emph{space} classes coincide: $NPSPACE = PSPACE$.
  \end{exampleblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Randomized classes: BPP, RP, and ZPP}
  \begin{block}{BPP / RP / ZPP}
    Problems solvable in polynomial time by an algorithm that flips coins, with
    \textbf{two-sided error} (BPP), \textbf{one-sided error} (RP), or \textbf{zero
    error but random running time} (ZPP $=$ RP $\cap$ coRP).
  \end{block}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Known: $\mathbf{P \subseteq ZPP \subseteq RP \subseteq BPP \subseteq PSPACE}$.
    \item Widely \textbf{conjectured} (not proven) that $BPP = P$: every efficient
    randomized algorithm can, in principle, be derandomized -- a major research theme
    connecting complexity theory and pseudorandomness.
    \item You will meet this whole family concretely in \textbf{Week 15} (randomized
    algorithms): primality testing, randomized quicksort, min-cut.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked example: Freivalds' algorithm (an RP-style check)}
  \textbf{Problem:} given $n\times n$ matrices $A, B, C$, is $C = AB$? Computing $AB$
  directly costs $O(n^\omega)$ (next section!). \textbf{Freivalds (1977):} pick a
  random $r \in \{0,1\}^n$ and just check $A(Br) \overset{?}{=} Cr$ -- $O(n^2)$ time.
  \[
    A = \begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix},\;
    B = \begin{pmatrix}5&6\\7&8\end{pmatrix},\;
    C = \begin{pmatrix}19&22\\43&50\end{pmatrix} (= AB),\;
    r = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}
  \]
  \[
    Br = \begin{pmatrix}11\\15\end{pmatrix}, \quad
    A(Br) = \begin{pmatrix}1(11)+2(15)\\3(11)+4(15)\end{pmatrix} =
    \begin{pmatrix}41\\93\end{pmatrix}, \quad
    Cr = \begin{pmatrix}41\\93\end{pmatrix}. \; \checkmark
  \]
  Now suppose someone claims $C' = \left(\begin{smallmatrix}19&22\\43&51\end{smallmatrix}\right)$
  (one wrong entry): $C'r = \binom{41}{94} \ne \binom{41}{93}$ -- \textbf{caught}.
  \begin{exampleblock}{The guarantee}
    If $AB \ne C$, a random $r$ catches it with probability $\ge \tfrac12$. Repeat $k$
    times independently: error probability $\le 2^{-k}$.
  \end{exampleblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Counting complexity: \#P and the permanent}
  \begin{block}{\#P}
    Instead of ``does a solution exist?'' (NP), \#P asks \textbf{``how many solutions
    are there?''} -- e.g., \#SAT: how many satisfying assignments does $\varphi$ have?
  \end{block}
  For a $2\times 2$ matrix $M = \left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)$,
  compare two almost-identical-looking formulas:
  \[
    \det(M) = ad - bc \qquad \text{vs.} \qquad \mathrm{perm}(M) = ad + bc.
  \]
  With $M = \left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{smallmatrix}\right)$:
  $\det(M) = 6 - 1 = 5$, but $\mathrm{perm}(M) = 6 + 1 = 7$.
  \begin{exampleblock}{A striking contrast}
    $\det(M)$ is computable in polynomial time (Gaussian elimination, or the same
    machinery as matrix multiplication, next section). Valiant (1979) proved computing
    $\mathrm{perm}(M)$ exactly is \textbf{\#P-complete} -- provably as hard as counting
    \emph{all} satisfying assignments of a SAT formula -- even though, for a 0/1 matrix,
    the permanent is just ``the number of perfect matchings'' in a bipartite graph
    (Weeks 10--11). Two formulas that differ only by a sign; wildly different
    difficulty.
  \end{exampleblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{P vs.\ NP: the \$1{,}000{,}000 question}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $P$ vs.\ $NP$ is one of the seven \textbf{Clay Millennium Prize Problems}
    (\$1{,}000{,}000 for a correct proof either way). Only one has been solved so far
    (the Poincar\'e conjecture, by Grigori Perelman, 2003).
    \item Almost every theorist believes $P \ne NP$ -- but belief is not proof, and the
    problem has resisted every attack for over 50 years.
    \item \textbf{Why you should care as a 2nd-year student:} every time you face a new
    problem in industry or research, ``is this secretly NP-hard?'' is one of the first
    questions worth asking, \emph{before} you spend a week trying to write a fast
    exact algorithm.
  \end{itemize}
  \begin{alertblock}{Looking ahead}
    Week 12 makes all of this precise: formal reductions, Cook--Levin, and a toolkit for
    proving your own problems NP-hard.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\section{Recent Advances I: Matrix Multiplication After Strassen}
% =================================================================

\begin{frame}{Recap: Strassen's trick (1969)}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item You saw last week that Karatsuba traded one multiplication for extra additions
    to beat the $\Theta(n^2)$ grade-school bound.
    \item Volker Strassen applied the \emph{same philosophy} to \textbf{matrix
    multiplication}: multiplying two $2\times 2$ block matrices needs only \textbf{7}
    multiplications (of the blocks), not the obvious \textbf{8}.
    \item Recursively applied to $n\times n$ matrices:
    \[
      T(n) = 7\,T(n/2) + O(n^2) \;\Longrightarrow\; T(n) = \Theta(n^{\log_2 7}) \approx
      \Theta(n^{2.807}),
    \]
    beating the schoolbook $\Theta(n^3)$ -- the first improvement in recorded history.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked example: Strassen's seven products}
  Let $A = \left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{smallmatrix}\right)$,
  $B = \left(\begin{smallmatrix}5&6\\7&8\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. Direct computation:
  $AB = \left(\begin{smallmatrix}19&22\\43&50\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ (8 multiplications).
  \textbf{Strassen's seven products:}
  \begin{small}
  \begin{align*}
    p_1 &= a_{11}(b_{12}-b_{22}) = 1(6-8) = -2, &
    p_2 &= (a_{11}+a_{12})b_{22} = 3(8) = 24, \\
    p_3 &= (a_{21}+a_{22})b_{11} = 7(5) = 35, &
    p_4 &= a_{22}(b_{21}-b_{11}) = 4(2) = 8, \\
    p_5 &= (a_{11}+a_{22})(b_{11}+b_{22}) = 5(13) = 65, &
    p_6 &= (a_{12}-a_{22})(b_{21}+b_{22}) = (-2)(15) = -30, \\
    p_7 &= (a_{11}-a_{21})(b_{11}+b_{12}) = (-2)(11) = -22. & &
  \end{align*}
  \end{small}
  \textbf{Combine (only additions from here):}
  \[
    c_{11} = p_5{+}p_4{-}p_2{+}p_6 = 19, \;\;
    c_{12} = p_1{+}p_2 = 22, \;\;
    c_{21} = p_3{+}p_4 = 43, \;\;
    c_{22} = p_5{+}p_1{-}p_3{-}p_7 = 50.
  \]
  Matches $AB$ exactly $\checkmark$ -- using \textbf{7}, not 8, multiplications.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The exponent $\omega$: a 50-year race}
  Define $\omega$ as the smallest exponent such that $n\times n$ matrices can be
  multiplied in $O(n^{\omega+\varepsilon})$ time for every $\varepsilon > 0$.
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
    \toprule
    Year & Result & Bound on $\omega$ \\
    \midrule
    1969 & Strassen & $2.807\ldots$ \\
    1990 & Coppersmith--Winograd & $2.376$ \\
    2014 & Le Gall & $2.3728639$ \\
    2020--21 & Alman--Williams & $2.3728596$ \\
    2022 & Duan--Wu--Zhou & $2.3719$ \\
    2024 & Williams--Xu--Xu--Zhou & $2.371339$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Nobody knows the true value of $\omega$; many suspect $\omega = 2$ is
    achievable, but every attempt to prove it has stalled.
    \item These are all \textbf{asymptotic} (large-$n$) results with enormous constants --
    not (yet) practical for the matrix sizes you'd use in a homework assignment.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A different question: the small-case puzzle}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Separately from the asymptotic race: \textbf{exactly how many scalar
    multiplications are needed to multiply two $4\times 4$ matrices?}
    \item Applying Strassen recursively to a $4\times 4$ matrix (as two levels of
    $2\times 2$ blocks) uses $7\times 7 = \mathbf{49}$ multiplications.
    \item For over 50 years, \textbf{49} was the best known scheme for $4\times 4$
    matrices -- until 2022.
  \end{itemize}
  \begin{block}{Why anyone cares about ``49 vs.\ 47''}
    Small building blocks get used \emph{recursively}. Shaving multiplications off the
    base case improves the constant in the asymptotic bound every level of recursion --
    exactly like the jump from 4 to 3 recursive calls did for Karatsuba.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{AI-assisted discovery joins the search}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
    \toprule
    Year & Who / what & Result for $4\times 4$ \\
    \midrule
    1969--2021 & Strassen (recursive) & 49 multiplications \\
    2022 & DeepMind's \textbf{AlphaTensor} & 47 multiplications (mod-2 arithmetic) \\
    2024 & Kauers \& Moosbauer (JKU Linz) & 48 multiplications (standard arithmetic) \\
    2025 & DeepMind's \textbf{AlphaEvolve} & 48 multiplications (complex-valued) \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item AlphaTensor (Nature, 2022) reframed matrix multiplication as a single-player
    game and used reinforcement learning to search for low-multiplication schemes --
    the first improvement on Strassen's 4x4 case in 55 years.
    \item AlphaEvolve (2025) is a large-language-model-driven \emph{evolutionary code
    search}: it proposes, tests, and refines algorithm implementations directly.
  \end{itemize}
  \begin{alertblock}{The lesson}
    A problem being ``settled since 1969'' didn't mean it was actually settled --
    it meant nobody had looked hard enough, with the right tools.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\section{Recent Advances II: Shortest Paths Beyond Dijkstra}
% =================================================================

\begin{frame}{Dijkstra's algorithm (1956) and the sorting barrier}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Single-source shortest paths:} given a graph with $n$ vertices, $m$
    edges, and a source $s$, find the shortest distance from $s$ to every other vertex.
    \item Dijkstra's algorithm: repeatedly pick the \emph{closest unvisited} vertex,
    ``finalize'' its distance, and relax its outgoing edges.
    \item In 1984, Fredman and Tarjan's \textbf{Fibonacci heap} made this run in
    $O(m + n\log n)$ time -- matching a fundamental lower bound: any algorithm that
    finalizes vertices \emph{in sorted order of distance} cannot beat the time it takes
    to \textbf{sort} $n$ numbers.
  \end{itemize}
  \begin{exampleblock}{The ``sorting barrier''}
    For 40 years, this looked like the end of the road for general (real-weighted)
    graphs -- \emph{unless} you find a way to avoid sorting altogether.
  \end{exampleblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked example: Dijkstra finalizes in sorted order}
  \begin{columns}
  \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[
      vtx/.style={circle, draw, thick, minimum size=7mm},
      >=Stealth]
    \node[vtx] (s) at (0,0) {$s$};
    \node[vtx] (a) at (1.8,1.3) {$a$};
    \node[vtx] (b) at (1.8,-1.3) {$b$};
    \node[vtx] (t) at (3.6,0) {$t$};
    \draw[->, thick] (s) -- node[pos=0.45,above,font=\small]{2} (a);
    \draw[->, thick] (s) -- node[pos=0.45,below,font=\small]{5} (b);
    \draw[->, thick] (a) -- node[pos=0.5,right,font=\small]{1} (b);
    \draw[->, thick] (a) -- node[pos=0.45,above,font=\small]{7} (t);
    \draw[->, thick] (b) -- node[pos=0.45,below,font=\small]{2} (t);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}
  \end{column}
  \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}clll@{}}
    \toprule
    Step & Finalize & Relax & New tentative dist. \\
    \midrule
    1 & $s$ (0) & $s\to a$, $s\to b$ & $a{=}2$, $b{=}5$ \\
    2 & $a$ (2) & $a\to b$, $a\to t$ & $b{=}3$, $t{=}9$ \\
    3 & $b$ (3) & $b\to t$ & $t{=}5$ \\
    4 & $t$ (5) & -- & -- \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{exampleblock}{This \emph{is} the sorting barrier}
    Finalization order $s, a, b, t$ is exactly increasing distance order
    $0 < 2 < 3 < 5$. Dijkstra's algorithm is, structurally, sorting the vertices by
    distance as it goes -- so it cannot run faster than sorting allows.
  \end{exampleblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{2024: Dijkstra is ``universally optimal''}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Haeupler, Rozho\v{n}, T\v{e}tek, Hladík, and Tarjan (\textsc{FOCS} 2024 best
    paper) asked a different question: not ``what is the worst case over \emph{all}
    graphs,'' but \textbf{``is there one algorithm that is the best possible on
    \emph{every individual} graph structure?''}
    \item They designed a new heap data structure and proved: a variant of Dijkstra's
    algorithm is \textbf{universally optimal} -- for every fixed graph \emph{shape},
    no algorithm can beat it (for worst-case edge weights on that shape).
    \item A beautiful, 70-years-later vindication of Dijkstra's original 20-minute,
    pencil-free design.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{2025: breaking the sorting barrier}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Just one year later, Duan, Mao, and collaborators (\textsc{STOC} 2025 best
    paper) proved the opposite-sounding result: an algorithm that
    \textbf{beats} Dijkstra's $O(m + n\log n)$ \emph{worst-case} bound, on general
    directed, real-weighted graphs.
    \item Key idea: don't finalize vertices strictly by increasing distance.
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Group nearby frontier vertices into \textbf{clusters}; explore one
      representative per cluster instead of scanning the whole frontier.
      \item Use short, bounded bursts of the (usually slow) \textbf{Bellman--Ford}
      algorithm to find ``influential'' hub vertices worth exploring next.
    \end{itemize}
    \item Because vertices are no longer finalized in sorted order (unlike our
    $s,a,b,t$ example above), the sorting lower bound simply \textbf{does not apply}.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reconciling the two results}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{4.6cm}p{4.6cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{2024 (Haeupler et al.)} & \textbf{2025 (Duan, Mao et al.)} \\
    \midrule
    ``Best possible \emph{per graph shape},'' among algorithms that finalize vertices
    by increasing distance & ``Faster \emph{in the worst case over all graphs},''
    by abandoning distance-order finalization entirely \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{alertblock}{The teaching moment}
    Both statements are true. ``Optimal'' always means \emph{optimal with respect to a
    model and a metric}. Changing the model (what the algorithm is allowed to do) or the
    metric (worst-case-over-graphs vs.\ per-graph-shape) can change the answer --
    a theme you will see again with approximation ratios in Week 14.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\section{Recent Advances III: Coloring Graphs Near-Optimally}
% =================================================================

\begin{frame}{The problem: edge coloring}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Motivating story:} at an airport, model runways/taxiways/gates as
    vertices and each aircraft's planned route as a path connecting them. Color each
    edge (route segment) so that no two edges sharing a vertex share a color -- colors
    represent non-conflicting time slots.
    \item \textbf{Vizing's theorem} (1964): every graph can be edge-colored with at most
    $\Delta + 1$ colors, where $\Delta$ is the maximum vertex degree. Knowing \emph{how
    many} colors suffice is easy.
    \item \textbf{Actually finding} such a coloring quickly is a different, much harder
    story.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked example: when is $\Delta+1$ actually necessary?}
  \begin{columns}
  \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
  \centering \textbf{Star $K_{1,3}$}: $\Delta = 3$
  \begin{tikzpicture}[vtx/.style={circle,draw,thick,minimum size=6mm}]
    \node[vtx] (c) at (0,0) {};
    \node[vtx] (l1) at (-1.1,0.9) {};
    \node[vtx] (l2) at (0,1.3) {};
    \node[vtx] (l3) at (1.1,0.9) {};
    \draw[thick, red] (c) -- (l1);
    \draw[thick, blue] (c) -- (l2);
    \draw[thick, green!60!black] (c) -- (l3);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \\[4pt] \footnotesize 3 colors suffice ($=\Delta$): \textbf{Class 1}
  \end{column}
  \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
  \centering \textbf{Triangle $K_3$}: $\Delta = 2$
  \begin{tikzpicture}[vtx/.style={circle,draw,thick,minimum size=6mm}]
    \node[vtx] (x) at (0,0) {};
    \node[vtx] (y) at (1.3,0) {};
    \node[vtx] (z) at (0.65,1.1) {};
    \draw[thick, red] (x) -- (y);
    \draw[thick, blue] (y) -- (z);
    \draw[thick, green!60!black] (z) -- (x);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \\[4pt] \footnotesize \textbf{3} colors needed ($=\Delta+1$): \textbf{Class 2}
  \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{exampleblock}{Why the triangle needs an extra color}
    Every pair of the triangle's 3 edges shares an endpoint, so \emph{all three} must
    get different colors -- $\Delta=2$ colors are not enough. Vizing's theorem's
    ``$+1$'' is sometimes unavoidable, and telling class 1 from class 2 graphs is
    itself NP-hard in general.
  \end{exampleblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A 40-year plateau}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Vizing's own algorithm runs in $O(mn)$ time (recoloring a chain of edges can
    cascade across the whole graph).
    \item 1980s improvements brought this down to $O(m\sqrt n)$ -- and then progress
    \textbf{stalled for roughly 40 years}.
    \item ``The research stopped for a very long time \ldots\ many people believed that
    there's no better way,'' as one researcher put it.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{2024--2025: near-linear time, finally}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item 2024: two independent groups broke the 40-year plateau within days of each
    other: Assadi's $O(n^2)$-time algorithm, and a separate $O(m\sqrt[3]{n})$-time
    algorithm, later refined to $O(m\sqrt[4]{n})$.
    \item 2025: Assadi, Behnezhad, Costa, and collaborators combined forces and went
    much further -- a \textbf{near-linear} $O(m\log m)$-time algorithm, presented at
    \textsc{STOC} 2025.
    \item Since simply \emph{reading} the graph takes $\Omega(m)$ time, $O(m\log m)$ is
    within a single logarithmic factor of the \textbf{theoretical minimum possible}.
  \end{itemize}
  \begin{block}{The key new idea: ``priming''}
    Rather than trying to color the \emph{last, hardest} edge faster (the old approach),
    use randomization to color \emph{almost all} edges at once, leaving only a small,
    structured set of edges -- which can then be colored quickly and nearly
    simultaneously.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why these three stories matter}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Every one of these breakthroughs attacks a \textbf{classical, textbook}
    problem: matrix multiplication, shortest paths, edge coloring.
    \item Every one of these barriers stood for \textbf{decades} (40--55 years) and was
    widely believed to be permanent.
    \item Every one of these breakthroughs came from a genuinely \textbf{new idea} about
    combining old techniques (clustering, bounded Bellman--Ford bursts, randomized
    ``priming''), not from more computing power alone -- though AI-assisted search
    (AlphaTensor, AlphaEvolve) is now a serious new tool in the theorist's toolbox.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{Summary}
% =================================================================

\begin{frame}{Summary}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The complexity zoo} -- L, NL, P, NP, coNP, PSPACE, EXP (time/space),
    plus BPP/RP/ZPP (randomness) and \#P (counting) -- gives you a vocabulary for
    asking ``how hard, provably, is this problem, and with respect to what resource?''
    \item \textbf{Matrix multiplication:} Strassen's 7-product trick generalizes to an
    asymptotic exponent $\omega$ that keeps inching down (2.807 $\to$ 2.371), and
    AI-assisted search (AlphaTensor, AlphaEvolve) has now improved even the small,
    55-year-old $4\times 4$ case.
    \item \textbf{Shortest paths:} Dijkstra's algorithm was proved \emph{universally
    optimal} in 2024 -- and its worst-case bound was still \emph{beaten} in 2025, by
    an algorithm that refuses to sort. Both are correct; they optimize different things.
    \item \textbf{Edge coloring:} a 40-year-stalled problem fell to near-linear time in
    2024--2025 via a new ``priming'' idea.
  \end{enumerate}
  \begin{alertblock}{Looking ahead}
    The tools behind every one of these results -- recurrences, worst-case analysis,
    reductions, randomization -- are exactly what the rest of this course builds up,
    week by week.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}
